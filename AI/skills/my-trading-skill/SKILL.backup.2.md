## Comprehensive Rules & Guidelines (TradingView master guide)

Use this section as a front-door filter before chapter-level pattern detail.

### 1) Rules vs guidelines (priority order)

- Rules are hard requirements. If a rule is violated, even slightly, discard that count and switch to an alternate count.
- Guidelines are probabilistic reliability tools. They can fail in some cases, but frequent alignment increases confidence.
- Enforce analysis order: rules -> structure -> guidelines -> confluence.

### 2) Non-negotiable impulse rules

- Wave 2 never retraces beyond the start of Wave 1.
- Wave 4 never enters Wave 1 territory (standard impulse context).
- Wave 3 is never the shortest of Waves 1, 3, and 5.
- Impulses subdivide into five waves; Waves 1, 3, and 5 are motive internally.

### 3) Corrective-family gate checks

- Zig-zag: typically 5-3-5.
- Flat family: 3-3-5.
- Triangle family: 3-3-3-3-3 across A-B-C-D-E.
- Combinations: W-X-Y or W-X-Y-X-Z as chained corrective structures with connector X waves.

### 4) Diagonal handling

- Distinguish leading vs ending diagonal by position and internal structure rules.
- Keep overlap and channel constraints consistent with diagonal rules in the source guide.
- Reject diagonal labels that violate geometry or wave-size constraints.

## Chapter 3.2 baseline: Flat and Expanded Flat

### 1) What a flat correction is

- A flat correction is a three-wave corrective pattern labeled A-B-C.
- Internal structure is 3-3-5:
  - Wave A: corrective three-wave move
  - Wave B: corrective three-wave move
  - Wave C: motive five-wave move (impulse or ending diagonal)
- It is typically sideways and often feels "choppy."
- In a regular flat, Wave C usually does not move much beyond the Wave A extreme.

### 2) Core rules for regular flat

- Wave A and Wave B are both corrective.
- Wave C is impulsive (or ending diagonal) and completes the pattern.
- Wave C usually does not extend far below (or above, in bullish inversion) the Wave A extreme.
- Wave B and/or Wave C can briefly exceed prior local boundaries, creating false breakout behavior.
- Regular flat is common, but not the most common corrective consolidation pattern overall.

### 3) What an expanded flat is

- Expanded flat is a flat variant with an extended Wave B.
- Structure remains 3-3-5.
- Wave B moves beyond the start of Wave A (fake breakout behavior).
- Wave C then extends in the opposite direction and exceeds the end of Wave A.

### 4) Core rules for expanded flat

- Wave B must end beyond the beginning of Wave A.
- Wave C should unfold as an impulse or ending diagonal.
- Wave C must end beyond the Wave A extreme in the opposite direction (deeper than A in bearish framing).
- The pattern is corrective overall, despite Wave C being motive internally.

### 5) Practical behavior and trade context (flat family)

- Flats and expanded flats commonly trap traders through repeated fake breakouts.
- These structures are often noisy and rotational; patience is important during the pattern.
- Prioritize structure completion over early breakout chasing.
- Quality improves when the analyst can confirm A=3, B=3, C=5 and check B/A boundary rules.

### 6) Quick diagnostic checklist (flat-focused)

Use this flow:

1. Can the structure be labeled A-B-C with internal 3-3-5?
     
   - If no, do not force a flat label.
2. Are Wave A and Wave B both corrective?
     
   - If no, reassess as zig-zag, triangle, or combination.
3. Does Wave B stay within A-start (regular flat) or break beyond it (expanded flat)?
     
   - Inside/near A-start favors regular flat.
   - Beyond A-start favors expanded flat.
4. Is Wave C motive (impulse/ending diagonal) and does it match the variant's boundary rule?
     
   - If yes, flat probability increases.
5. Is price action unusually choppy with fakeouts at B/C turns?
     
   - If yes, this supports flat-family behavior.

## Chapter 3.3: Running Flat and Contracting Triangle

### 1) Running flat (last flat variant)

- Running flat is the **least common** flat type; it still uses the same **3-3-5** structure as other flats (A: 3, B: 3, C: 5).
- It is **easy to misread** because a **rising wedge** shape (in a bullish trend) often reads bearish to many traders; **confirmation often comes late**, nearer the end of the pattern—**trade the breakout** (per XForceGlobal framing on strong trends).
- **Typical context:** very **strong, fast** markets (the source highlights **Bitcoin**-style trends). A **rapid, extended Wave B** with a **relatively short Wave C** fits a market where the **primary trend is dominant**.

**Rules (bullish correction in an uptrend; mirror for bearish):**

- Wave **B** ends **above** the **start** of Wave A.
- Wave **C** ends **above** the **end** of Wave A (i.e. C does not undercut A’s extreme—“running” in the trend direction).
- **Often** Wave **C** is approximately the **same length** as Wave A.

**Charting note:** A **parallel channel** (upper bound through correction highs, lower bound through lows) **often** anchors the **Wave C low** (yellow channel lines in the reference diagrams).

### 2) Contracting triangle (symmetrical triangle)

- Triangles reflect **balance** between buyers and sellers: **five overlapping waves** with internal **3-3-3-3-3** structure (each of A–E is a three-wave corrective swing).
- The **contracting** triangle is the **most frequently seen** triangle form: **upper trendline slopes down**, **lower trendline slopes up** (lines converge).

**Rules:**

- **Five waves:** A–B–C–D–E (all legs **corrective** in character within the triangle).
- **Lines:** upper boundary **declining**, lower boundary **rising**.
- Wave **E** often **overshoots** the trendline; **E can itself be a triangle** (nested complexity allowed in practice per this framework).
- **Position in larger count:** triangles appear as **Wave 4, B, X, or Y**—**not** as **Wave 2 or A** (“never” in the cited rules).
- **Continuation:** a completed triangle **continues the prior dominant trend** (continuation pattern = main trend direction).

### 3) Quick diagnostic checklist (running flat vs expanded vs triangle)

1. **Running flat:** A-B-C with **3-3-5**, B **beyond** A’s origin, C **fails** to reach A’s extreme **in the counter-trend direction** (in bull: C low **above** A low). Wedge-like **compression** without five triangle legs.
2. **Expanded flat:** A-B-C **3-3-5**, B beyond A origin, but C **exceeds** A’s extreme **against** the larger trend (deeper/more extended than running flat’s C).
3. **Contracting triangle:** **Five** swings **A–E**, each subdividing **3-3-3-3-3**, bounded by **converging** trendlines; not a single ABC in isolation.

## Chapter 3.4: Barrier Triangle and Expanded Triangle

**Framing (from XForceGlobal):** Barrier and expanded triangles **share the same characteristics as the contracting triangle**—the practical difference is **overall shape**. Common classical-chart patterns (e.g. ascending triangle) sit underneath the Elliott labeling; learning the simple chart shapes helps stack advanced wave rules on top.

### 1) Shared triangle DNA (contracting family)

- Same **five-wave corrective overlap** concept as contracting triangles: legs **A–E**, internal **3-3-3-3-3** (per this guide’s triangle rules).
- **Typical position:** before the **last thrust** in the trend **one degree higher**—often **wave 5** of an impulse or **wave C** of an ABC correction (also **X** or **Y** in complex corrections such as **double-three** or **triple-three**).
- **Projection (working guideline):** the post-triangle thrust **often** travels about the **width of the widest part** of the triangle (measured across the formation).
- **Continuation expectation:** thrust **with** the trend that preceded the triangle (same as contracting triangle continuation idea).

### 2) Barrier triangle

**Rule vs contracting (key distinction):** In a barrier triangle, **one boundary is horizontal**—per the guide, either the **B–D line** or the **A–C–E line** is horizontal (the other leg still slopes so the triangle is a **contracting variant**, not two parallel rails).

- **Same as contracting** except **waves B and D end at essentially the same level** (flat barrier at one side of the pattern).
- **Everyday chart analog:** the classical **ascending triangle** (horizontal **resistance** with **rising support** / higher lows) is explicitly called out as the common real-world shape—price taps the flat ceiling at **B** and **D**, sweeps higher lows at **A, C, E** on the rising lower line, then **breaks out** in the direction of the dominant trend (bullish in an uptrend).

**Diagram labels (reference slides):** Pattern context is often shown starting from a prior motive leg (e.g. **“3, A, C”** at the top) and resolving after **E** into **“4, B, X, Y”**—i.e. barrier triangles appear where other triangles do: **4 / B / X / Y**, not as **2** or **A** per Chapter 3.3 rules.

### 3) Expanded triangle (“expanding triangle”)

**Rule vs contracting (key distinction):**

- **Waves C, D, and E** each **exceed** the end of the **prior same-direction subwave**:
  - C moves beyond A’s extreme,
  - D beyond B’s extreme,
  - E beyond C’s extreme (same-directional progression), producing **wider swings** as time moves forward.
- **Line geometry:** a line through **B and D** **diverges** from a line through **A and C** (upper and lower boundaries **spread apart** instead of converging).

**Behavior read:** volatility **expands** inside the pattern; termination and breakout still follow the **triangle position** and **trend-continuation** logic above—diagrams label endings with **“4, B, X, Y”** consistent with other triangle placements.

### 4) Quick diagnostic checklist (contracting vs barrier vs expanded)

1. **Contracting:** both rails **converge**; neither A–C–E nor B–D is required to be flat; B and D **do not** share the same flat level unless it is a barrier subtype.
2. **Barrier:** **flat** resistance or support side (B ≈ D **or** A–C–E collinear on a horizontal); other side still angled—classic **ascending**/**descending** triangle silhouette.
3. **Expanded:** **diverging** boundaries; waves **C, D, and E** each extend past the prior **same-direction** pivot—**not** converging compression.

## Chapter 3.5: Double Three (W-X-Y)

**Source framing (XForceGlobal, *Elliot Waves Complete Guide*):** A **double three** is a **complex corrective** pattern—notation **W-X-Y**. It strings together **two** simple corrective structures (each a "three": zig-zag, flat, or triangle family) joined by a connecting **X** wave. **Triple three** (**W-X-Y-X-Z**) adds a **third** simple correction and a **second** connecting **X** (covered in Chapter 3.6 below).

### 1) Composition and labeling

- **(W):** First corrective "leg" of the combination—often a **flat** (including **expanded flat**) or **zig-zag**, shown in references as internal **A-B-C** where applicable.
- **(X):** **Connecting** wave between the first and second three. It may take any corrective form, but is **usually** in the **zig-zag** form.
- **(Y):** Second corrective "leg"—again a simple pattern such as **flat**, **zig-zag**, or **triangle** (when (Y) is a triangle, use the internal **a-b-c-d-e** structure).

**Visual hallmark:** the guide highlights **sideways consolidation**—price drifts in a **horizontal** box rather than a deep, sharp double zig-zag-style reversal.

### 2) Rules (combination constraints)

- **Zig-zag** and **triangle** each occur **at most once** inside this W-X-Y combination (per the guide's combination rules).
- **Triangles appear only as Wave (Y)** in this rule set (the final leg), not as (W) or (X).
- **Alternation:** single legs tend to alternate in form (for example, a flat followed by a triangle is called out as more common than two flats back-to-back), though two flats in a row is still possible.
- **Double three vs double zig-zag:** the **orientation** differs—double three is more **horizontal/sideways**, while double zig-zag corrects **sharper** and more against the major trend.

### 3) Purpose, placement, and trading context (from the article)

- Main purpose of double (and triple) threes: expand the **duration** of the correction (time/consolidation), not necessarily the depth.
- Common placement: often in the **shallower part of Wave 4**.
- Completion cue: watch for **triangle completion** at the end of (Y) or the **final wave C** of the second three to anticipate continuation of the prior impulsive trend direction (educational framing only; not advice).
- "Almost equal segments" / accumulation: the article notes double threes often show segments (including the X wave) that are **similar in size**, resembling an **accumulation/consolidation** phase.
- Double three vs double flat (in the article): double three **has no channelling component** in their framework, and the **X wave** typically retraces **more than 61.8%** back into the first correction's territory.
- Timeframe caution: on **daily/weekly/monthly** timeframes, the author warns that prolonged holding can be expensive in FX-style instruments due to swap costs (context-dependent; educational only).

### 4) Example combinations (from screenshots you provided)

- **Expanded flat – X – triangle**: (W) expanded flat (3-3-5), (X) corrective connector, (Y) triangle with **five legs** (a-b-c-d-e), then continuation.
- **Expanded flat – X – zig-zag**: same W-X-Y logic with (Y) as a zig-zag/ABC corrective structure.
- **Flat – X – triangle**: a non-expanded flat first leg followed by triangle (Y).
- **Flat – X – zig-zag**: a simpler double-three where both corrective legs are flat/zig-zag family members.

Note: some slide captions in the wild can be inconsistent (for example, a drawn triangle may be informally labeled "zig-zag"). The safer identification is the **five-wave triangle** internal structure and **converging/dividing** geometry.

### 5) Quick diagnostic checklist (double three)

1. Can the zone be split into **two** corrective "threes" plus a connecting **X**?
2. Is the overall behavior more **sideways/horizontal** than a steep double zig-zag?
3. If there is a **triangle**, is it only in **(Y)**? If there is a **zig-zag**, is it only **once** in the whole combination? If there is a second occurrence, reconsider the label.
4. Do the legs show a reasonable **alternation** of form (flat vs zig-zag vs triangle) more often than repeating the same thing?
5. Does the completion of **(Y)** (triangle e or final C of a flat/zig-zag) align with the expected **resumption** of the prior impulsive direction?

## Chapter 3.6: Triple Three (W-X-Y-X-Z)

**Source framing (XForceGlobal, *Elliot Waves Complete Guide*, Chapter 3.6 "Triple Three"):** The **triple three** is **very similar to the double three**—it is just **more elongated and extended**. The overall count is **(W)-(X)-(Y)-(X)-(Z)**: **three** simple corrective patterns separated by **two** connecting **(X)** waves.

### 1) Composition and labeling

- **(W):** First simple corrective leg (e.g. flat, expanded / irregular flat, zig-zag)—internally **A-B-C** where applicable.
- **(X):** First **connecting** wave between **(W)** and **(Y)**—**usually** a **zig-zag** in practice, but may take **any** corrective form (this flexibility makes consolidation look **messy**).
- **(Y):** Second simple corrective leg—typically **flat**, **zig-zag**, or other non-triangle three.
- **(X):** Second connecting wave between **(Y)** and **(Z)**—same flexibility as the first **(X)**.
- **(Z):** Third and **final** simple corrective leg—completes the combination (**may** be a **triangle**, ending the whole structure).

### 2) Rules (from the article and diagrams)

- **Triangle placement:** A **triangle is only valid as wave (Z)**—the **final** leg of the triple three—and therefore **marks the end** of the correction.
- **Building blocks:** The broad family includes flat / zig-zag / triangle, but in this chapter's working rule, keep **(W)** and **(Y)** as non-triangle threes and allow triangle only on **(Z)**.
- **Alternation:** Legs **usually show alternation** in type across the sequence (a tendency, not a rigid requirement).
- **Illustrated combinations:** Examples shown include **flat-X-zig-zag-X-triangle**, **zig-zag-X-irregular flat-X-flat**, **flat-X-expanded flat-X-zig-zag**, and **expanded flat-X-zig-zag-X-triangle**.

### 3) Sideways context and trading psychology ("final notice about corrections")

From the chapter (educational framing; not advice):

- **Long sideways corrections** are where many traders lose money because the market lacks a clear trend.
- Price can show repeated **false breakouts** and messy action; often the better default is to wait for a clearer trend-resumption signal after completion.
- Higher-timeframe ranges can be traded, but the source frames this as mostly for experienced traders; otherwise this period is often treated as accumulation / preservation context.

### 4) Relationship to double three

- **Double three:** **W-X-Y** (two threes, one **X**).
- **Triple three:** **W-X-Y-X-Z** (three threes, two **X** waves).
- Both are largely **sideways/horizontal** consolidations, with triple three usually longer in time.

### 5) Quick diagnostic checklist (triple three)

1. Can the zone be split into **W, X, Y, X, Z** (three corrective legs + two connectors)?
2. Is behavior mostly **sideways/range-bound** rather than a steep multi-segment zig-zag?
3. If a **triangle** appears, is it only in **(Z)** (final leg)?
4. Do both **(X)** waves behave like connectors (often zig-zag, but not required)?
5. Does completion of **(Z)** (triangle e or final C) align with expected prior-trend continuation?

## Chapter 4.1: Alternation (guideline / application)

**Source framing (XForceGlobal, *Elliot Waves Complete Guide*, Chapter 4.1 "Alternation"):** After the rules for individual patterns in earlier chapters, Chapter 4 shifts to applications and guidelines for linking patterns into a bigger count. Chapter **4.1** introduces **alternation**: related corrective legs in the same larger structure often differ in character. This is a **probability guideline**, not a hard rule.

**Series context:** The chapter overview also lists **4.2 Channeling**, **4.3 Psychology**, **4.4 Fib-Ratio**, **4.5 Motive Wave Multiples**, and **4.6 Corrective Wave Multiples**. This skill adds full coverage for **4.1**, **4.2**, **4.3**, and **4.4**.

### 1) Alternation between Wave 2 and Wave 4 (inside a five-wave impulse)

**Plain language:** In a completed impulse **(1)-(2)-(3)-(4)-(5)**, corrective waves **(2)** and **(4)** frequently alternate. One tends to be **simple / sharp / deeper in price**, while the other is **more complex / sideways / longer in time** (often shallower in price).

**From the guide (macro wording):**

- Alternation appears between the two corrective macro swings in a completed five-wave sequence: one often develops as a simple correction (often ABC), the other as a more complex corrective structure, while the broad five-wave picture remains intact.
- If **Wave 2** is simple ABC, probability favors **Wave 4** as more complex. If **Wave 2** is complex, probability often favors a simpler **Wave 4**.

**Typical chart read (illustrated in your screenshots):**

- **Wave 2** is often sharp and can retrace deeply (example guideline around **61.8%** of wave **(1)**).
- **Wave 4** is often sideways and more time-consuming (flat, triangle, double three, triple three), with shallower retracement (example guideline around **38.2%** of wave **(3)**).

**Note in the material:** Wave 2 is usually zig-zag-like and corrects much of the prior move; Wave 4 is often flat/triangle/double-three/triple-three and tends to extend duration (accumulation/balance phase).

### 2) Alternation inside a larger corrective structure (A)-(B)-(C)

Alternation can also happen **inside** corrective waves themselves, not only between primary Wave 2 and Wave 4.

Two variations shown in the guide:

1. **Variation 1:** **(A)** is an ABC **flat**, then **(B)** is an ABC **zig-zag**.
2. **Variation 2:** **(A)** is an ABC **zig-zag**, then **(B)** is an ABC **flat**.

In both cases, the pattern finishes with a classic **(C)** that is a five-wave countertrend move in this framework.

### 3) Quick diagnostic checklist (alternation)

1. Do waves **2** and **4** show different correction character (simple/sharp vs complex/sideways)?
2. Does Fib context roughly fit a deep **2** (~61.8% of **1**) vs shallow **4** (~38.2% of **3**) as a soft clue?
3. Inside **(A)-(B)-(C)**, does **(A)** vs **(B)** alternate between flat and zig-zag forms?
4. Keep invalidation tied to wave-structure rules. Missing alternation alone does not invalidate a count.

## Chapter 4.2: Channeling (Parallel Channels in Impulses)

**Source framing (XForceGlobal, *Elliot Waves Complete Guide*, Chapter 4.2 "Channeling"):** Channeling uses **parallel channels** to frame impulse-wave boundaries and improve probability on where waves may terminate.

### 1) Temporary channel (after Wave 3)

- Once you identify **Wave 1, 2, and 3**, draw an upper line through the highs of **1** and **3**.
- Draw a **parallel** lower line through the low of **2**.
- This temporary channel projects likely **Wave 4** support at the lower rail.

### 2) Complete channel (for Wave 4 and Wave 5)

- If Wave 4 respects/lives near the lower rail, the same channel becomes the complete impulse channel.
- **Wave 5** is then often projected toward the upper rail.
- Treat this as a **guideline**, not a guaranteed endpoint.

### 3) Redraw rule when Wave 4 misses

- If Wave 4 does not terminate near the lower boundary in the initial setup, redraw by connecting the ends of **Wave 2 and Wave 4**.
- Then draw a parallel line from the end of **Wave 3** to estimate Wave 5.

### 4) Channel invalidation

- If price breaks the channel prematurely and continues in a way that violates the working impulse read, the channeling idea is invalid.

### 5) Quick diagnostic checklist (channeling)

1. Do we have credible pivots for **1, 2, 3**?
2. Is the channel built as **1–3 line + parallel through 2**?
3. Does **4** test/live around the lower rail?
4. Does **5** project toward the upper rail?
5. If not, did we apply the **2–4 redraw + parallel from 3** before rejecting the count?

## Chapter 4.3: Market Psychology

**Source framing (XForceGlobal, *Elliot Waves Complete Guide*, Chapter 4.3 “Market Psychology”):** Market psychology is treated as a core part of Elliott Wave work: price is read as the **collective reflection of beliefs** of participants—**mass psychology** cycling between **pessimism and optimism**. A clear read of psychology **by wave position** (impulse **1–5** and corrective **A-B-C**) helps anticipate typical behaviors even in simple five-wave and **abc** structures.

### 1) Waves 1–5 — market psychology (from the guide)

- **Wave 1** is often **hard to identify**: the **prior trend** may still look intact; action can be **choppy**, with **overlapping** sub-waves and **wedge / diagonal**-like shapes rather than clean impulse clarity.
- **Wave 2** **retraces deeply** relative to **Wave 1**; most participants still **expect the old trend** to resume and **miss** that a **new** trend may be forming.
- **Wave 3** is **almost always the largest** move—often the **first** leg newer Elliott readers recognize confidently. It marks **trend change as “done”** in behavior terms: after a **higher low** in **Wave 2**, **Wave 3** delivers the **higher high** (bullish framing; mirror for bearish). It is **frequently extended**, with **strong momentum** and **no major** interruptions.
- **Wave 4** is often **easier to anticipate** in character because it **alternates** with **Wave 2** (see Chapter **4.1**): it tends to take **more time**, feel **more complex**, and generate **false** technical **signals** as the crowd whipsaws.
- **Wave 5** is the **final push** with the **primary** trend. It commonly arrives with **lower volume** and **clear divergence** on tools like **RSI** (**bearish** divergence in a bull **5**; **bullish** in a bear **5**).

### 2) ABC correction — market psychology (from the guide)

- **Wave A** can unfold in **either three or five waves** depending on context. Psychologically it reflects **exhaustion** created from the previous **five-wave** leg; it is regularly not clearly visible as many traders think it is a normal pullback of the dominant trend.
- **Wave B** can take many shapes and is known for being a **“bull-trap”** (bearish ABC mirror: “bear-trap”). Many long the top of the B wave. **B** can retrace a lot lower than Wave A, or it can shoot up fast—sometimes even **above the last high**—catching traders on the wrong side as a **fake breakout** occurs. In textbook terms, **B** often shows up as the **right shoulder** of a **head-and-shoulders** formation.
- **Wave C** is **impulsive** and has **five waves against the dominant trend**. It usually has the length of **Wave A**, but an extension to the **1.618** fib or even greater is possible, making them **fast and devastating** moves as they punish impulse waves.

### 3) Quick diagnostic checklist (psychology)

1. **Position:** Are we labeling **1–5** inside an impulse or **A-B-C** after a completed **five**? Psychology bullets apply to the **active** degree.
2. **Crowd default:** Early trend (**1**), deep pullback (**2**), “obvious” trend (**3**), complex chop (**4**), exhaustion (**5**)—does the **story** fit without forcing the count?
3. **ABC trap:** Is **A** dismissed as “just a pullback”? Is **B** sucking in counter-trend bets (trap / fake breakout / shoulder)? Is **C** setting up as the sharp five-wave completion?
4. **Confirmation:** Tie psychology claims to **structure** (subdivisions, alternation, channeling); psychology **guides** narrative, it does **not** override rules.

## Chapter 4.4: Fibonacci Ratios

**Source framing (XForceGlobal, *Elliot Waves Complete Guide*, Chapter 4.4 "Fibonacci Ratios"):** Fibonacci levels are used to estimate probable turning zones and wave targets inside Elliott structures. The chapter divides practical use into two categories:

- **Retracement** (yellow in the slides): probable targets for corrective waves.
- **Extensions** (purple in the slides): probable targets for the next impulsive wave.
- **Anchor points:** major fib links are drawn from key swing highs/lows.

### 1) Core retracement/extension guidelines in this framework

The chapter lists these guideline ranges:

- **Wave 2 of Wave 1:** 50%, 61.8%, 76.4%, or 85.4% retracement of Wave 1.
- **Wave 3 from Wave 2:** 161.8% or 261.8% extension.
- **Wave 4 of Wave 3:** 38.2% to 61.8% retracement of Wave 3.
- **Wave 5 from Wave 4:** 61.8%, 100%, or 123.6% extension of Wave 4.

The screenshots also show the common teaching pair:

- **Wave 2 near ~0.618 of Wave 1** (deeper pullback).
- **Wave 4 near ~0.382 of Wave 3** (shallower pullback).

### 2) Practical usage sequence

1. Identify active wave position first (do not use fib in isolation).
2. Measure retracement or extension from the correct prior wave leg.
3. Use fib **zones**, not a single exact price.
4. Combine fib with structure checks, alternation, and channeling.
5. Prefer confirmation from local candlestick behavior before acting.

### 3) Invalidation and constraints (chapter notes)

- **Wave 2 cannot retrace beyond 100% of Wave 1.**
- **Wave 3 cannot be the shortest impulsive wave.**
- **Wave 4 must not overlap Wave 1 territory** in a standard impulse.

### 4) Alternation-guided priority from the chapter

The chapter ties fib usage back to alternation:

- Wave 2 usually retraces more deeply (often around 61.8%).
- Wave 4 is usually shallower (often around 38.2%).
- If candlestick confirmation appears, a 50% retest entry can be possible.

Treat these as probability guidelines, not standalone proof.

## Chapter 4.5: Fibonacci Lengths (Motive Wave Multiples)

**Source framing (XForceGlobal, *Elliot Waves Complete Guide*, Chapter 4.5 "Fibonacci Lengths"):** This chapter focuses on motive-wave length targeting after wave position is identified. The central idea is to project the next impulse/correction leg by mapping it to prior completed motive legs using Fibonacci multiples. In this framework, **Wave 1 has no predetermined target** because it starts the trend.

### 1) High-level mapping from the chapter

- **Wave 2 length** is measured as a retracement of **Wave 1**.
- **Wave 3 length** is projected as an extension of **Wave 1** from the Wave 2 pivot.
- **Wave 4 length** is measured as a retracement of **Wave 3**.
- **Wave 5 length** has **variation** and depends on whether **Wave 3** is extended.
- **Wave 1:** no fixed fib target in advance (origin wave).

### 2) Wave-by-wave target guidelines (from link + screenshots)

**Wave 2 (relative to Wave 1):**

- Common retracement zones: **50%** or **61.8%** of Wave 1.
- Deeper alternatives shown: **76.4%** and **85.4%**.
- Measurement anchor in the slides: connect Wave 1 swing low to swing high, then project retracement for Wave 2.

**Wave 3 (relative to Wave 1):**

- Primary target zones: **161.8%** and **261.8%** extension of Wave 1.
- Chapter note: rare extended cases can travel **beyond 261.8%** (extension within an extension).
- Most common termination zones in the visuals are around those two levels.

**Wave 4 (relative to Wave 3):**

- Typical retracement zones: **23.6%** or **38.2%** of Wave 3.
- This keeps Wave 4 shallower than Wave 2 in common alternation scenarios.

**Wave 5 (conditional logic):**

- If **Wave 3 is not extended** (e.g., less dominant vs standard extension behavior), Wave 5 may extend and can relate to the move from the **start of Wave 1 to the top of Wave 3**, with common projection around **161.8%** in the screenshot examples.
- If **Wave 3 is extended**, Wave 5 more often relates to **Wave 1** by:
  - **100% of Wave 1**,
  - **161.8% of Wave 1**, or
  - **261.8% of Wave 1**.
- Practical takeaway from the chapter text: Wave 5 is usually the most variable motive target and should be treated as a zone with scenario branches.

### 3) Decision flow for live counting (chapter-consistent)

1. Confirm structural position first (do not project fib length without a valid wave count).
2. Compute Wave 2 retracement of Wave 1.
3. From Wave 2 pivot, project Wave 3 as 1.618/2.618 x Wave 1.
4. After Wave 3 confirms, project Wave 4 at 0.236/0.382 of Wave 3.
5. For Wave 5, branch by whether Wave 3 appears extended; map both conservative and extended outcomes.
6. Mark all projections as **zones**, then combine with channeling, alternation, and subdivisions.

### 4) Validation and invalidation guidance for 4.5 usage

- Use 4.5 targets only when impulse structure rules remain valid (Wave 2 not beyond Wave 1 start, Wave 3 not shortest, Wave 4 no overlap into Wave 1 in standard impulse).
- A fib miss alone is not an automatic count invalidation; structural rule breaks take priority.
- If Wave 3 exceeds standard extension zones materially, keep alternate Wave 5 scenarios active rather than forcing a single endpoint.

## Chapter 4.6: Corrective Wave Multiples (A-B-C Fibonacci Lengths)

**Source framing (XForceGlobal, *Elliot Waves Complete Guide*, Chapter 4.6 "ABC Fib Lengths" / "Corrective Wave Multiples"):** Chapter 4.5 applied Fibonacci **length** relationships to **motive** waves **1-5**; Chapter **4.6** applies the same **multiple** idea to **corrective** **A-B-C** (and to each zig-zag inside **W-X-Y** when the combination is a **double zig-zag**). The guide ties this material to **Chapter 3.1** (corrective waves) as prerequisite context.

**Primary reference:** [Elliot Waves Complete Guide | Chapter 4.6 - "ABC Fib Lengths"](https://www.tradingview.com/chart/BTCUSD/YaMxVSes-Elliot-Waves-Complete-Guide-Chapter-4-6-ABC-Fib-Lengths/) (TradingView, XForceGlobal).

### 1) Core idea: Wave C length vs Wave A

Across the patterns below, **Wave C** commonly relates to **Wave A** by one of these **length multiples** (same direction as C; measure **A** as one full leg, then compare **C**'s traveled distance):

- **1.0** - C **equals** A (equality; often the **default** in a **zig-zag** in this framework).
- **1.618** - C extends to **161.8%** of A.
- **0.618** - C terminates near **61.8%** of A.

Use these as **probability zones** after the count is structurally plausible - not as proof of a label.

### 2) Zig-zag correction (5-3-5)

In a **zig-zag** A-B-C (internally **5-3-5** per Chapter 3.1 framing), **Wave C** is **often** about the **same length** as **Wave A** (**1.0**). **1.618** and **0.618** **also** appear as **common** C-vs-A outcomes for fixing C's magnitude.

### 3) Double zig-zag (W-X-Y)

In a **W-X-Y** that is a **double zig-zag**, the **same C-vs-A multiple set** (**0.618**, **1.0**, **1.618**) applies **separately** to the **first** zig-zag **(W)** and the **second** zig-zag **(Y)** - i.e. each **A-B-C** leg can show those relationships between its own **A** and **C**.

### 4) Flat correction (including extended flat)

- **Regular / sideways flat:** A, B, and C are **often** **similar in extent** in teaching diagrams ("quite equal"), still read with **structural** 3-3-5 rules from Chapter 3.2.
- **Extended flat:** **Wave C** **often** reaches about **161.8% (1.618)** of **Wave A** when measured **from the start of Wave B** to the end of **Wave C** (the guide's **extended-flat** measurement note).

### 5) How to identify A-B-C targets after a five-wave impulse (companion screenshot method)

The following is a practical **Fibonacci placement** recipe from the series' **"How to IDENTIFY the Corrective Waves for an A-B-C Correction"** slide set (after a completed **4-5** impulse segment). **Bearish correction after a bull 1-5** is shown below; **mirror** anchors for a **bullish** ABC after a bear impulse.

**Wave A (first corrective leg):**

- Draw **retracement** from **Wave 4** -> **Wave 5** (the last impulse swing into the top).
- Common **A** termination zone: **38.2%** or **50%** retracement of that **4-5** segment.

**Wave B (counter-trend bounce):**

- Draw **retracement** from **Wave 5** -> **end of Wave A**.
- Common **B** termination zone: **38.2%** or **50%** retracement of **5-A**.

**Wave C (second impulse-style leg of the correction):**

- Use **extension / projection** from the **A** leg into **C**.
- Common **C** zones: **138.2%** and **161.8%** relative to that A-based projection.

Treat these **identification** levels together with Chapter **4.6** **C vs A** multiples (0.618 / 1.0 / 1.618) as **confluent zones**, not single-price mandates.

### 6) Order of operations and invalidation (from Chapter 4.6 text)

- **Measure last:** Fibonacci **length** work is **secondary** to **structure**. If the **overall count** is **invalid** or **rules** are broken, **do not** expect reliable wave **measurement** targets.
- Fibonacci relationships are **complementary** to Elliott rules, not a substitute.

### 7) Quick diagnostic checklist (corrective multiples + ABC identification)

1. **Structure first:** Is the correction labelable as **zig-zag**, **flat**, **W-X-Y** double zig-zag, etc., per Chapters **3.1-3.2** and combinations?
2. **C vs A:** For zig-zag / each zig-zag in W-X-Y, does **C** land near **0.618x**, **1.0x**, or **1.618x** **A**?
3. **Extended flat:** If flat family with **B** beyond **A**'s origin, check **1.618xA** for **C** measured **B-start -> C-end** per the guide.
4. **Post-5 ABC identification:** After **4-5**, map **A** with **4-5** fib (**38.2 / 50**), **B** with **5-A** (**38.2 / 50**), **C** with A-based **extension** (**138.2 / 161.8**).
5. **Invalidate on structure:** Misaligned fib **alone** does not invalidate a count if wave **rules** still hold; broken **rules** invalidate the **count**, not just the target.

## Response behavior for this skill

- **Flats:** classify the working count as regular flat, expanded flat, **running flat**, or invalid.
- **Triangles:** confirm or reject **contracting**, **barrier**, or **expanded** triangle reads using **5 legs**, **3-3-3-3-3**, **line geometry** (converging / one flat rail / diverging), and **position (4/B/X/Y only)**—with combination caveats: in **W-X-Y**, triangles only as **(Y)**; in **W-X-Y-X-Z**, triangles only as **(Z)**.
- **Double three:** classify **W-X-Y**, name each leg's pattern, confirm **X** as the connector, enforce **one zig-zag / one triangle** cap where applicable, and apply the **(Y)-only triangle** rule.
- **Triple three:** classify **W-X-Y-X-Z**, name **(W)/(Y)/(Z)** and both **(X)** connectors, enforce **triangle only as (Z)**, and mention alternation / messy connector behavior.
- **Alternation (4.1):** state whether Wave **2 vs 4** (or **(A) vs (B)** inside an ABC) shows simple-vs-complex alternation; treat **~61.8%** and **~38.2%** as **soft context** from the guide, not hard rules.
- **Channeling (4.2):** state whether the count is using a **temporary** or **complete** channel, verify **1–3 line + parallel through 2**, and call out **Wave 4 lower-rail** and **Wave 5 upper-rail** expectations; if 4 misses, apply the **2–4 redraw + parallel from 3** rule.
- **Psychology (4.3):** map the active leg to typical crowd behavior from **Wave 1–5** or **A-B-C**: choppy **1**, deep retracement/old-trend bias **2**, largest/momentum **3**, alternating complex **4**, exhaustion/divergence **5**; and for ABC: **A** exhaustion/misread, **B** trap/fake breakout/shoulder, **C** five-wave impulsive completion; always keep narrative subordinate to verified structure.
- **Fib ratios (4.4):** classify whether the setup is **retracement** (correction) or **extension** (impulse projection), then map chapter levels as zones: **2 of 1 = 50/61.8/76.4/85.4**, **3 from 2 = 161.8/261.8**, **4 of 3 = 38.2-61.8**, **5 from 4 = 61.8/100/123.6**; use **0.618 Wave 2** vs **0.382 Wave 4** as alternation context, not proof alone.
- **Fib lengths (4.5):** when relevant, compute motive-wave multiples in sequence: **Wave 2 from Wave 1 (50/61.8 or 76.4/85.4)**, **Wave 3 from Wave 1 (161.8/261.8, occasionally beyond)**, **Wave 4 from Wave 3 (23.6/38.2)**, and **Wave 5 with branch logic** (if 3 extended -> 5 often tied to Wave 1 at 100/161.8/261.8; if 3 not extended -> allow 5-extension scenarios including 1-to-3 based projections in this guide).
- **Corrective multiples (4.6):** when relevant, state **C vs A** targets using **0.618 / 1.0 / 1.618** for **zig-zag** and each leg of a **double zig-zag W-X-Y**; for **extended flat**, cite **1.618xA** for **C** measured **from start of B to end of C**; optionally combine with **post-5 ABC identification** fibs (**A:** 38.2/50 of **4-5**; **B:** 38.2/50 of **5-A**; **C:** 138.2/161.8 extension from **A**); subordinate all measurements to valid wave structure per Chapter 4.6.
- Always provide **one clear invalidation** for the active count.
- If uncertain, give the **closest alternate** (e.g. zig-zag, combination, different triangle subtype) and what would **confirm** it.
- Include **structural** evidence (subdivision counts) and **rule** evidence (boundary and position rules).
